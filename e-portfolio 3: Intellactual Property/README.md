Government media release: Albanese Government to ensure Australia is prepared for future copyright challenges emerging from AI

Open artefact/source
Rowland, M 2025, ‘Albanese Government to ensure Australia is prepared for future copyright challenges emerging from AI’, media release, Attorney-General’s portfolio, 26 October, viewed 5 September 2026.



Summary

The Australian Government's announcement on its response to copyright issues caused by AI, in October 2025, clarifies its opinion. It prevents a sweeping exception for text and data mining that would mean artificial intelligence companies wouldn't need to take permission to use Australian creators' works. Rather, the Government focuses on fair licensing, transparency in the law when it comes to AI-generated content and enforcement that is more cost-effective (Rowland 2025).


Justification & Reflection

This artefact was selected to challenge the way I think about the start of copyright issues. I used to think of copyright only with relation to copying completed products but with the training of AI, it can be a problem at an earlier stage. This seems like a part of Week 7's discussion on incentivizing creators and increasing access to knowledge more broadly. As an ICT professional, if I should make use online resources for model training or commercial purposes should not take it for granted that such is readily available.



Artefact 2: AI Training and the Limits of Fair Use


News article: Thomson Reuters wins AI copyright “fair use” ruling against one-time competitor
Image: 

Open artefact/source
Brittain, B 2025, ‘Thomson Reuters wins AI copyright “fair use” ruling against one-time competitor’, Reuters, 11 February, viewed 5 September 2026.


Summary

The judge ruled in favor of Thomson Reuters, as Ross Intelligence made a copy of Westlaw material when creating a competing AI legal-research service, Reuters reports. The court dismissed Ross Intelligence's 'fair use' argument, and the case will play a key role in future discussions about the use of copyrighted material for building AI systems (Brittain 2025).


Justification & Reflection

This is an artefact that I chose because it came as a surprise for me to discover that it was ok to use copyrighted material to create a new product. It was revealed in the case that with regard to competition and the effects of the market, the effect of purpose is relevant as well. It directly connects to Week 7 of discussing fair use, copyright infringement and the difference between being technically feasible and being ethically responsible. Sweeping copyright under the carpet is something that I'd like to see in future software projects be handled with settlement or verification for ownership, before development.




Artefact 3: Scholarly Journal Article – Generative AI and Copyright

Scholarly journal article: Text and Data Mining, Generative AI, and the Copyright Three-Step Test

Open artefact/source
Senftleben, M 2026, ‘Text and data mining, generative AI, and the copyright three-step test’, IIC – International Review of Intellectual Property and Competition Law, vol. 57, no. 1, pp. 67–107, doi:10.1007/s40319-026-01680-2.


Summary

Senftleben (2026, p. 67) will look at the copyright exceptions for uses of text and data mining in the development of the generative artificial systems, and consider whether they are compatible with the international principles of copyright. It is demonstrated in the article how rights reservations, opt-outs, and remuneration can help balance the interests of AI innovators and the rights-holders.


Justification & Reflection

I picked this one because the piece was a catalyst for me to reject the idea of an all-creator approach as the only possible power dynamic for AI. It demonstrates the effectiveness of using intermediate mechanisms to safeguard innovation and rights in the governance process. This follows the theme of Week 7 which was that protection of intellectual property is a balance between creativity and social welfare. From an ICT point of view, there is a design element of mentioning "provenance", "licensing" and "rights management" instead of talking about them after deployment.

Summary

The Week 7 workshop examined the monkey-selfie copyright dispute and used it to discuss human authorship. The case showed that owning or setting up a technological tool does not necessarily determine who is legally considered the author. The workshop connected this issue to uncertainty about copyright ownership in works produced with generative AI (CQUniversity 2026).


Justification & Reflection

I chose this workshop idea because it challenged my assumption that the person operating a tool automatically owns everything it produces. Comparing the monkey selfie with AI-generated images made me question how much human creative contribution is required before copyright arises. As a future ICT professional, developers increasingly use AI-generated text, code and media. I would check authorship, licence terms and commercial-use permissions rather than assume AI outputs belong to users.
