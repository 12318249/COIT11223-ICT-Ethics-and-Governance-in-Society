Summary:

The IBM Technology video, How to Implement AI Ethics, covers ways organizations can incorporate principles of the highest ethical standards into the AI development cycle—before using the technology. The video explains some of the key tenets of operation: fairness, explainability, transparency and privacy protection. It serves as an example of the application of these tools and techniques to detect algorithmic bias and model drift, including using a bias detection toolkit (e.g. AI Fairness 360) and incorporating audit trails and human oversight.


Justification & Reflection:

Prior to this video and workshop, I thought ethics in AI was a topic primarily concerned with the privacy laws and securing personal information. I think I believed that if it's legal it's ethical. It was obvious in my mind that if the business was based on law, then the technology would also be. In my college days, however, I came across Utilitarianism and Kantianism ethics and the idea went to another dimension. Kantianism ethics taught me that all developers must bear in mind the question of respecting human dignity over humans being used to gather data, while Utilitarianism emphasized maximizing social good for the largest number of people as a whole.

My view of how these theories are applied in real-world software engineering showed me the concepts of fairness testing and bias detection in the IBM video. One case I personally know of is the use of automated hiring software, which has a secondary effect of expediting the hiring process, but which can, in fact, discriminate against certain minority groups and thus be in violation of the principle of fairness outlined by Kantianism. This made me feel very wary about my future endeavours as a Software Developer. Having accepted a core responsibility in terms of the ethical component that needs to ensure that technology does not have a negative impact on vulnerable people in society, I know that we are duty-bound to consider this issue. So I know that thinking about the issue is a duty, because I am accepting that ethical AI is not just a technical aspect, it is a need, and I want to ensure that technology doesn't cause harm to vulnerable people in society.

Summary:

The Australian legislation to ban social media under the age of 16 is covered by the article, Australia Approves Social Media Ban on Under-16s, written by the BBC News. The law's purpose is to minimize online harm, predatory practices and mental health problems attributed to addictive algorithms. The government demands age verification systems and is set to impose stiff fines when social media companies fail to comply.



Justification & Reflection:

I consider how my thinking needs to evolve to account for an article that made me question the role of government in curbing access to technology to help safeguard young people. Prior to our discussions and workshop, I had a conception of social media regulation as a political debate on corporate rules vs personal freedoms. When I considered applying ethical theories to the issue, however, I was able to see the issues at a deeper level.

Having less social media available to young teenagers, can be justified on a Utilitarian basis, if the benefit to society outweighs any mental health risks that it poses. At the other extreme, a Kantianism view caught me out that just because someone is a minor, they have to be treated with respect and autonomy, and consequently it was disrespectful and degrading to sort something out on their behalf by having them sign up for an age verification process. The thing I found most notable was that I felt very cramped as I realized that there is no easy “right” answer. This demonstrated to me that there are challenging balances to make between keeping people safe and safeguarding personal liberty within tech governance.

Reference: Sridhar, U 2025, ‘AI ethics: Integrating transparency, fairness, and privacy in AI development’, Applied Artificial Intelligence, vol. 39, no. 1, pp. 101–122, https://doi.org/10.1080/08839514.2025.2463722



Summary:

Sridhar (2025) in the peer-reviewed journal article AI Ethics: Integrating Transparency, Fairness, and Privacy in AI Development discusses ethics in the context of technology policy landscapes around the world. The author performs comparative analysis of international standards addressing major ethical problems to the implementation of AI.Comparative evaluation of upcoming international standards (such as the EU AI Act and US NIST framework) on addressing the key ethical challenges in deployment of AI. The paper summarizes practical mechanisms that can be developed and used to prevent automated bias and individual privacy concerns like regular algorithm audits, the incorporation of fairness-enhancing training data, and accountability protocols like the explicit identification of data sources that inform automated decisions. 


Justification & Reflection:

I believed that tech firms were somehow able to self-regulate with their own establishment policies, before I read this paper and possibly took part in class discussions. I was convinced that ethical issues were primarily technical problems that programmers could eventually recognise and resolve. But it was the work of Sridhar (2025, p. 105) that made me understand that there are many voluntary guidelines that can only be effective when enforced. 

When I read through this article with Social Contract Theory in mind, I changed my perspective to how I see tech companies in the context of a civilization. I now see that companies have an unspoken duty towards society that software is safe, transparent and fair in return for being publicly trusted and run. I was challenged by this framework and made myself feel more responsible as an IT student to study this framework. I now recognise the importance of having rules, standards and legal clarity and independent oversight to ensure that technology remains in line with public good and human rights.
